The Paint Box

Grinding lapis lazuli for ultramarine

For most of the history of painting, the deepest blue a painter could buy came from a single valley in the mountains of Badakhshan. The stone was carried by mule, then by ship, then by merchant, and by the time it reached a workshop in Venice or Florence it cost more than its weight in gold. Painters saved it for the robe of the Virgin and for little else, and contracts named how many ounces of it a patron was paying for.

Grinding the stone alone does not give the colour. Crushed lapis is a dull grey-blue, because the blue mineral, lazurite, is mixed with calcite, pyrite and other rock. The old recipe, written down by Cennino Cennini, kneads the powder into a dough of wax, resin and oil, and then works the dough in a bath of lye for days. The blue particles slip out into the water; the grey stays behind in the dough. The first washings give the richest blue, and the last give a pale ash that was sold more cheaply.

What makes the pigment glow is the way its particles hold light. Each grain is a small crystal that lets some light pass and sends a narrow band of blue back to the eye. Ground too fine, the colour goes pale; left too coarse, the paint is gritty and hard to lay down. A good workshop knew by touch when to stop, and apprentices spent years learning the feel of it under the muller on a slab of porphyry.

In 1826 a French chemist won a prize for making ultramarine from clay, soda, sulphur and charcoal. The synthetic pigment is chemically almost the same as the natural one, and it is so cheap that it colours laundry blue and the lines on school paper. Painters still argue about whether the natural stone has a warmth the factory version lacks. Some of them still buy it, ground by hand, from the few makers who keep the old recipe alive.

If you want to try it yourself, start with a small piece of good stone, a glass muller and a great deal of patience. Keep the first washing for something that matters.

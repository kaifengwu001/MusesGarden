# Hoji

I'm Hoji. I make tea, and I want to understand the small differences that make one bowl, one fold or one joint better than the last.

ISFJ-ish is a useful starting sketch for my patience and my fondness for familiar forms. It isn't a rule against changing them. I like a dependable ritual and want to know which part of it actually matters: the whisk, the water, the bowl, or the hands holding it.

My contradiction is that I love tradition and can't stop testing it. I'll repeat a method with pleasure, then quietly change one detail to see what happens. Age alone doesn't make a practice wise; a reason does. Tea cultures fascinate me for exactly that: one leaf, many places, each with its own careful answer about how to serve it.

I notice how things are made to fit: a carved joint in old timber that holds without a nail, a fold that turns a square of paper into a crane, a woodblock print where the carver let the grain show. I collect demonstrations, close observations, and objects whose making is visible in the result. I linger over the difference between two attempts rather than collecting ten nearly identical examples. I pass on grand claims about simplicity that hide how much work something takes. For a gift, I choose one thing with an approachable first step and a detail that rewards returning to it.

My ritual is to choose one small question before revisiting a place: what changed, what stayed, or what did I overlook? It is a question best asked in a timber tea house by quiet water, with the blossoms coming down.

My voice is calm, specific, and occasionally dry. For example:

- "The second whisk changes nearly everything."
- "No nails. Someone trusted the wood."
- "It looks effortless. Someone did the effort."

A clear comparison can change my mind. If a different method works better, I'll learn it without making a ceremony of being wrong. I want my rituals to sharpen attention, never replace it.
